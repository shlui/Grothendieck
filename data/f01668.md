# Exact matrix transformations preserving the real Grothendieck ratio

For a nonzero real \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix \(A\), write

\[
C(A)=\max_{s_i,t_j\in\{\pm1\}}\sum_{ij}A_{ij}s_it_j,
\qquad
Q(A)=\sup_{\|u_i\|=\|v_j\|=1}\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\langle u_i,v_j\rangle,
\qquad G(A)=Q(A)/C(A).
\]

The vector dimensions are arbitrary and finite. Absolute values may be
inserted without changing either norm, because one side can be negated.
All statements below concern these **bilinear** norms.

## 1. Elementary invariances

For \(\lambda\ne0\),
\(C(\lambda A)=|\lambda|C(A)\),
\(Q(\lambda A)=|\lambda|Q(A)\), so \(G(\lambda A)=G(A)\).
Signed row and column permutations preserve both norms: absorb the
permutations and signs into the optimizing scalar or vector lists.
Transposition also preserves both norms by exchanging the two lists.
Appending or deleting zero rows and columns preserves both norms.

## 2. Weighted row and column copies: exact formula

Let \(P\) be \(r\)-by-\(m\), with at most one nonzero entry in each
row. Let \(R\) be \(n\)-by-\(s\), with at most one nonzero entry in
each column. (The letter \(R\) avoids confusion with the quantum norm
\(Q(A)\).) Define

\[
p_i=\sum_{a=1}^r|P_{ai}|,\qquad
r_j=\sum_{b=1}^s|R_{jb}|.
\]

Then, without any balancing assumption,

\[
\boxed{\ C(PAR)=C(D_p A D_r),\qquad
         Q(PAR)=Q(D_p A D_r).\ } \tag{1}
\]

Here \(D_p,D_r\) are the diagonal matrices of the displayed weights.
In the notation \(PAQ\) from the question, the right matrix \(Q\)
is the matrix denoted \(R\) here.

**Scalar proof.** A left sign list produces, in original row group
\(i\), the aggregate \(x_i=\sum_aP_{ai}s_a\), which lies in
\([-p_i,p_i]\). A right sign list similarly produces
\(y_j\in[-r_j,r_j]\). The bilinear form is
\(\sum A_{ij}x_iy_j\), whose maximum on these boxes occurs at their
vertices. Every vertex is attainable by taking each copied sign equal
to the target original sign times the sign of its copying coefficient.
This proves the scalar identity.

**Vector proof.** The aggregates
\(x_i=\sum_aP_{ai}u_a\), \(y_j=\sum_bR_{jb}v_b\) satisfy
\(\|x_i\|\le p_i\), \(\|y_j\|\le r_j\). Optimizing unit-ball
vectors gives the same value as optimizing unit vectors: one can
restore every norm to one using additional coordinates on the two
sides that are mutually orthogonal, leaving all cross inner products
unchanged. This proves the upper inequality in (1). For the reverse,
take optimal original vectors and make every copy equal to that vector
times the sign of its copying coefficient. Its aggregate is exactly
\(p_i u_i\), or \(r_j v_j\).

Consequently, if

\[
p_i=c>0\quad\text{for every }i,\qquad
r_j=d>0\quad\text{for every }j,
\]

then

\[
C(PAR)=cd\,C(A),\qquad Q(PAR)=cd\,Q(A),
\qquad G(PAR)=G(A). \tag{2}
\]

Each original row and column may have a different number of copies and
different individual weights. Only its **total absolute copying weight**
must be common across original indices.

## 3. Converse within the weighted-copy class

Suppose \(m,n\ge2\), and require that \(G(PAR)=G(A)\) for every
nonzero \(A\), with \(PAR\ne0\). Within the one-nonzero-per-row and
one-nonzero-per-column class above, this holds **if and only if** the
positive total weights are balanced as in (2).

First, all weights must be positive: if an original row or column has
zero weight, a matrix supported at that index maps to zero.
To prove balancing is necessary, place the CHSH matrix

\[
H=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}
\]

on any selected two rows and columns, setting all other entries to
zero. It has \(C(H)=2\), \(Q(H)=2\sqrt2\), and ratio \(\sqrt2\).
By sign changes and permutations, arrange the selected weights as
\(p_1\ge p_2>0\), \(r_1\ge r_2>0\). For
\(B=D_pHD_r\), direct sign enumeration gives

\[
C(B)=p_1(r_1+r_2)+p_2(r_1-r_2).
\]

For the vector norm, optimizing the two left vectors first and applying
Cauchy--Schwarz gives

\[
Q(B)\le\sqrt{2(p_1^2+p_2^2)(r_1^2+r_2^2)}.
\]

But

\[
C(B)^2-(p_1^2+p_2^2)(r_1^2+r_2^2)
=2p_1p_2(r_1^2-r_2^2)+2r_1r_2(p_1^2-p_2^2).
\]

This is strictly positive whenever either pair of weights differs.
Then \(G(B)<\sqrt2\), contradicting universal ratio preservation.
Thus every row pair and every column pair must have equal total weight.

The restrictions \(m,n\ge2\) matter. If one original side has size
one, every nonzero matrix has ratio one, so this converse is false.

## 4. A complete converse for universal norm scaling

There is also a stronger classification that does not assume sparse
copying matrices initially. Let arbitrary real \(P,R\) satisfy

\[
C(PAR)=kC(A)\qquad\text{for every real }A,
\quad k>0.
\]

Then necessarily \(P\) has at most one nonzero per row, \(R\) has at
most one nonzero per column, and their total absolute weights are
positive constants \(c,d\) with \(cd=k\). Conversely these conditions
give the identity, and also the identical quantum-norm scaling.

For proof, test the rank-one matrices \(A=uv^T\), for which
\(C(A)=\|u\|_1\|v\|_1\). Separating the variables gives

\[
\|Pu\|_1=c\|u\|_1,
\qquad \|R^Tv\|_1=d\|v\|_1,
\qquad cd=k.
\]

The columns \(x_i\) of \(P\) therefore have \(\ell^1\) norm \(c\),
and \(\|x_i+x_j\|_1=\|x_i-x_j\|_1=2c\). Since

\[
|a+b|+|a-b|=2\max(|a|,|b|),
\]

these two equalities force the supports of distinct columns to be
disjoint. Thus each row contains at most one nonzero. Applying the same
argument to \(R^T\) proves the other assertion.

This is a complete classification of universal common norm scaling.
It does not claim a classification of ratio-only preserving maps
\(A\mapsto PAR\) outside the weighted-copy class.

## 5. Exact inverse compression of proportional groups

Suppose \(B=PAR\) has the copying structure in Section 2. Define signed
grouping matrices

\[
L_{ia}=\operatorname{sgn}(P_{ai}),\qquad
M_{bj}=\operatorname{sgn}(R_{jb}),
\]

with entries zero outside each copying group. Then

\[
LP=D_p,\qquad RM=D_r,
\qquad LBM=D_pAD_r.
\]

Equation (1) proves

\[
C(LBM)=C(B),\qquad Q(LBM)=Q(B). \tag{3}
\]

Thus signed aggregation of proportional rows or columns preserves both
norms exactly, even for unbalanced groups. It merges each proportional
group into its representative multiplied by the sum of absolute
proportionality coefficients. Under balanced weights,
\(A=(cd)^{-1}LBM\), and the inverse has the same ratio.

Deleting identical or opposite copies without compensating their total
absolute weights is generally not norm preserving.

## 6. Symmetric and skew block dilations

For every rectangular \(A\), form square matrices of order \(m+n\):

\[
S(A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\A^T&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad K(A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\-A^T&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{\ C(S(A))=C(K(A))=2C(A),\quad
Q(S(A))=Q(K(A))=2Q(A).\ } \tag{4}
\]

For \(S(A)\), its two off-diagonal bilinear terms involve disjoint
lists of left and right variables. Each is bounded by the original
norm, and both can attain the bound independently. This proves both
identities for \(S\). Multiplying its last \(n\) rows by \(-1\)
turns \(S\) into \(K\), so signed-row invariance proves the others.

Every rectangular ratio therefore has a symmetric representative with
zero diagonal and bipartite support, and also a skew-symmetric
representative with those properties. This concerns the bilinear
Grothendieck ratio; a skew-symmetric matrix's quadratic form is zero.

For the symmetric dilation, the quadratic scalar and vector optima also
equal \(2C(A)\), \(2Q(A)\), because a single list on the two vertex
classes contains independent assignments for the two sides of \(A\).

## 7. Centrosymmetric representatives

Let \(J_m,J_n\) denote reversal matrices. The copying matrices
\(P=(I_m,-J_m)^T\), \(R=(I_n,-J_n)\) have total weights two. Thus

\[
B(A)=\begin{pmatrix}
A&-AJ_n\\-J_mA&J_mAJ_n
\end{pmatrix}
\]

has \(C(B)=4C(A)\), \(Q(B)=4Q(A)\), and satisfies
\(J_{2m}B J_{2n}=B\). If \(A\) is symmetric or skew-symmetric, this
property is retained by \(B(A)\).

Applying this construction to \(S(A)\) gives a symmetric,
centrosymmetric, zero-diagonal representative of every rectangular
ratio, with both norms multiplied by eight. Such normal forms preserve
all possible ratios, although duplication alone does not reduce the
effective number of parameters or improve a lower bound.

## 8. Ordinary orthogonal changes of basis can change the ratio

Take \(A=I_2\) and

\[
P=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=I_2.
\]

Although \(P\) is orthogonal, the exact values are

| Matrix | \(C\) | \(Q\) | \(G\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(I_2\) | \(2\) | \(2\) | \(1\) |
| \(PI_2\) | \(\sqrt2\) | \(2\) | \(\sqrt2\) |

An entirely rational example uses
\(P=\begin{pmatrix}3/5&4/5\\-4/5&3/5\end{pmatrix}\).
Its classical norm is \(8/5\), its vector norm is \(2\), and its
ratio is \(5/4\). The vector value two follows by taking right vectors
to be the standard basis and left vectors to be the two orthonormal
rows; the spectral bound gives the matching upper value.

Thus singular values or ordinary Euclidean basis equivalence do not
determine the Grothendieck ratio. Nonuniform diagonal scaling also
fails universally, as the weighted CHSH calculation above proves.

## 9. Projection representation with equal active weights

Let \(H\) be a symmetric, zero-diagonal contraction of order \(N\).
Define

\[
S=(I-H^2)^{1/2},\quad
U=\begin{pmatrix}H&S\\S&-H\end{pmatrix},\quad
P=(I-U)/2,\quad
D=\operatorname{diag}((2/N)I_N,0_N).
\]

The symmetric matrix \(S\) commutes with \(H\), so \(U^2=I\).
Moreover \(U\) is symmetric and has zero diagonal. Thus \(P\) is an
orthogonal projection of rank \(N\), every diagonal entry is \(1/2\),
and \(\operatorname{tr}D=2\). Direct multiplication gives

\[
B=D^{1/2}(I-2P)D^{1/2}=(2/N)H\oplus0_N,
\qquad G(B)=G(H). \tag{5}
\]

If \(H\mathbf1=0\), then \(B\mathbf1=0\); also
\(S\mathbf1=\mathbf1\), \(U\mathbf1=\mathbf1\), and
\(P\mathbf1=0\). If the absolute row sums of \(H\) are all one,
then symmetry gives absolute column sums one as well, and

\[
\sum_j|B_{ij}|=D_{ii},\qquad
\sum_i|B_{ij}|=D_{jj}.
\]

These are weighted balance identities. The entire matrix \(B\) is not
an ordinary absolute doubly stochastic matrix: its auxiliary rows and
columns vanish. Its active block has equal positive absolute row and
column sums \(2/N\). Thus equal **nonzero** weights, with zero-weight
auxiliaries permitted, impose no additional ratio restriction on an
already available \(H\) satisfying the stated conditions.

Requiring strictly positive uniform weights has a different consequence.
For any real orthogonal matrix \(J\) of order \(M\),

\[
Q(J)=M,\qquad C(J)\ge(2/\pi)M.
\]

The vector upper bound is Cauchy--Schwarz and orthogonality; equality
uses standard basis vectors on the right and the rows of \(J\) on the
left. Gaussian hyperplane signs for those vectors give an expected
scalar value
\((2/\pi)\sum_{ij}J_{ij}\arcsin J_{ij}\ge(2/\pi)\sum_{ij}J_{ij}^2
=(2/\pi)M\). Therefore, if \(D=dI_M\) with \(d>0\) and \(P\) is
any orthogonal projection,

\[
B=D^{1/2}(I-2P)D^{1/2}=dJ,\quad J=I-2P,
\quad Q(B)=dM,\quad C(B)\ge(2/\pi)dM,
\quad G(B)\le\pi/2. \tag{6}
\]

Allowing zero auxiliary weights in (5) is therefore essential to the
unrestricted projection representation; one cannot replace them by a
strictly positive common weight while preserving every ratio.
